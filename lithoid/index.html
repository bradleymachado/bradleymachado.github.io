Work / W.02

Lithoid

2026

Habitat, design and manufacturing. A cast-concrete nesting surface for cliff swallows on the North Branch of the Chicago River at River Park, Chicago, built from three interlocking modules cast in 3D-printed molds and turned to the orientation that keeps the nests from overheating.

Type

Tools
Rhino · Grasshopper · Ladybug · Wallacei X · Enscape · 3D printing · Concrete casting

Cast-concrete nesting panel system for cliff swallows, River Park, Chicago

01 / Client
Species

The client is the cliff swallow, Petrochelidon pyrrhonota — a small, compact songbird 5–6 in long with a 12 in wingspan, broad rounded wings and a short clipped tail, a red head, black cap and light tan body. It breeds across the United States and western Canada and winters in South America. In the Chicago region, River Park included, swallows arrive between late April and mid-May and nest from May to July. Research began with three candidate species — chimney swift, black-capped chickadee and cliff swallow — and narrowed to the swallow, a colonial bird that already builds on cliffs, bridges and the sides of buildings.

Behavior

Cliff swallows build gourd-shaped mud nests in colonies on vertical surfaces, gathering mud from nearby water that is also where they find insects. The roosting brief reduces to two sets of considerations: a vertical surface, cover and environmental protection; and a place near water, elevated and with room for a colony. Height keeps the nests out of reach of land animals, and an undetectable position keeps them from bigger birds. Reuse matters most — offspring do better when parents return to an existing nest than when they rebuild, so a surface that holds old nests season after season is part of the brief rather than a side effect.

02 / Concept
Starting point

Initial concepts took a direct approach to housing: a smokestack of stacked spheres, soffit beams of interlocking nest blocks and a breeze-block screen wall whose frames took feeder, house and planter inserts. A sketchbook note asked whether chimneys could be made in the park, with Greg Lynn's Blob Wall Pavilion pinned beside it as inspiration. The breeze blocks were the most developed of these, but they set a rigid grid, and the work turned to the spaces in the built environment where housing could be placed and to ways of breaking that grid with more organic shapes.

Surface

Ripples on the surface of the water describe the creek's currents, but life's activities register there too. Those ripples, thrown as light onto a vertical concrete wall above the river, became the design concept: a flat sheet pulled into an S-curve and then folded into a horizontal lip, giving the overhangs that swallows read as vertical security. A first version divided the surface into square tiles, with small holes in the face where birds could build within. The cast test panel, a 4 × 5 grid of tiles, carries one continuous channel across its joints. The sketch notes put the shift plainly — the surface is the solution.

03 / Component
Modules

Component 2.0 cut the ripples into a grid and let the pieces accrete into a frame. Three modules make the system. Module 1, "Burrito", is the standard pipe — a straight S-profile piece with pin holes along its top shelf. Module 2, "T-Bone", creates an intersection of multiple lines, so a run of pipe can branch and close into cells. Module 3, "Plate", expands the spaces between the barrels, creating habitat; its ripple noise pattern references the riparian surface. Pipe and junction draw the lines of the frame, and the plate fills the habitat between them.

Assembly

The pieces mount on a rail system. Slots in each component drop onto a rectangular-tube rail frame, and the plate may be slipped onto the rail in any orientation, which allows a complex, free-form facade rather than a repeated tile. Because each piece registers on the rail and to its neighbors through pin holes, the frame grows by accretion, one module at a time. Mounted between posts and placed along the shoreline, the finished surface stands as a habitat wall at the water's edge — the vertical surface, cover and height the swallows look for, built where mud and insects are close at hand.

04 / Site
Location

The site is River Park, Chicago, where the North Shore Channel joins the North Branch of the Chicago River. The proposed panels sit on the east bank within sight of the River Park canoe and kayak launch, along the 0.25 mi reach between W Foster Ave and W Argyle St. Earlier site studies looked through the park for vertical spaces near water — retaining walls, a pumping station and bridges along the river corridor — the kinds of structure the swallows already colonize. River Park is on the birds' own calendar as well: they arrive here between late April and mid-May.

Orientation

Orientation was tested as an environmental question. A Ladybug chain — weather file, cumulative sky matrix, incident radiation — fed Wallacei X, which rotated the Lithoid geometry with radiation as the objective to minimize. Cumulative incident radiation came to 4839.3 kWh/m² facing north (0°), 5200.8 kWh/m² facing west (90°) and east (270°) and 5116.7 kWh/m² facing south (180°). The optimum is south-facing (180°) at 5116.7 kWh/m². North receives the least, but care must be taken to prevent convection to the rear of the panel. The stakes are the chicks: mud nests act as a thermal heat sink, and a site that looks ideal can lethally overheat them — an ecological trap (Imlay, Nickerson and Horn 2019, Canadian Journal of Zoology 97(5): 429–435).

05 / Manufacturing
Molds

Each module is cast in its own multi-part mold, modeled around the finished piece and 3D-printed on a Bambu Lab printer. The molds come apart in nested layers: inner shells that carry the ripple, edge pieces that close the sides, outer housings that hold the assembly together and pins that register the parts and form the pin holes. The Plate's tray mold has the ripple noise pattern printed into its floor. The housings are windowed and slotted, and the layers are printed in contrasting colors — green inner shells inside magenta or blue housings — so each part reads clearly in the exploded drawings.

Casting

The molds are clamped, filled with concrete and demolded. Held together with bar clamps and wooden strips, a mold keeps its shape while the concrete sets; the printed parts are then pulled apart and the casting comes away with its ripple and pin holes in place. The first cast test panel, at the second milestone, showed that the relief could run continuously across tile joints. The module castings that followed carry the same relief in one piece, from the S-profile pipe demolded on the bench to the Plate poured into its clamped tray.

Result

Lithoid ends as a working fabrication system and a proposal for the riverbank. Three modules — pipe, junction and plate — interlock on a rail into a free-form habitat surface, each cast in concrete from its own 3D-printed mold. Set on the east bank at River Park and turned south-facing (180°), the panel offers cliff swallows a vertical surface near water with cover, elevation and room for a colony, in an orientation chosen so the nests do not become a trap. In the closing visualization a single panel stands in the goldenrod at the water's edge, its cells taken by mud nests.
